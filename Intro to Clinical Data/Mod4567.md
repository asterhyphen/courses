
# Intro to Clinical Data – Modules 4–7

# Module 4: Creating Analysis-Ready Datasets

## Patient-Feature Matrix

***Definition:*** Tabular data where each row represents a patient and each column represents a feature.

### Preparing Data
1. **Access Data:** Use SQL to retrieve records and JOIN tables.
2. **Standardization:** Scale features to a common range (0–1 or mean 0, SD 1).
3. **Feature Reduction:** Remove unnecessary or redundant features.
4. **Handle Missing Data:** Remove or estimate missing values.
5. **Feature Engineering:** Create useful features from existing data.

### Feature Reduction

***Reasons:***
- **Missingness:** Feature absent for many patients.
- **Sparsity:** Many features absent for one patient.
- **Redundancy:** Highly correlated features.
- **Privacy:** Sensitive patient information.
- **Efficiency:** Too many features slow analysis.

***PCA (Principal Component Analysis):*** Mathematical technique that combines features to reduce dimensions, but may reduce interpretability.

## Missing Data

***Definition:*** Values absent from the dataset, sometimes because they were never measured or considered necessary.

***Solutions:***
- **Deletion:** Remove rows or columns with missing values (may introduce bias).
- **Mean Imputation:** Replace missing values with column average.
- **KNN Imputation:** Estimate values using similar patients.
- **Multiple Imputation:** Create multiple estimated datasets to account for uncertainty.

**Rule:** Few missing values → consider imputation. Mostly missing → consider dropping the feature.

## Feature Engineering

***Definition:*** Creating new features by transforming or combining existing measurements.

***Examples:***
- BMI from weight and height.
- Comorbidity scores from multiple diseases.
- Counts, ratios, differences, and rates of change.
- Using glucose-test orders as a proxy for diabetes.

## Knowledge Graphs

***Definition:*** Organized collections of medical entities, synonyms, and relationships (also called ontologies).

***Contains:***
- Entities and their synonyms.
- Relationships such as **"is a kind of"**.
- Links between different medical terminologies.

***Uses:*** Standardize medical terms, identify synonyms, combine features, and improve clinical text mining.

***Examples:***
- **ICD:** Disease classification.
- **CPT:** Medical procedures.
- **RxNorm:** Medications.
- **LOINC:** Laboratory tests and observations.
- **UMLS:** Combines multiple medical knowledge graphs.

# Module 5: Unstructured Healthcare Data

## Clinical Text

***Definition:*** Medical notes written by healthcare professionals, often using abbreviations, incomplete sentences, and specialized terminology.

***Uses:*** Identify diseases, improve diagnosis coding, support research, and construct patient cohorts.

***Challenges:***
- **Negation:** "No diabetes" should not count as diabetes.
- **Context:** "Family history of cancer" does not mean the patient has cancer.
- Misspellings, abbreviations, and ambiguous terms.

## Natural Language Processing (NLP)

***Definition:*** AI techniques used to process human language.

***Main Steps:***
1. **Tokenization:** Split text into words and sentences.
2. **Parsing:** Identify grammatical structure.
3. **Named Entity Recognition (NER):** Identify medical terms and assign categories.
4. **Section Detection:** Identify sections of clinical documents.

***Clinical NLP Goal:*** Extract useful **positive, present mentions** while excluding negated and historical conditions.

- Knowledge graphs help find medical terms and synonyms.
- **NegEx:** Tool for detecting negation.

## Privacy & De-Identification

***PHI (Protected Health Information):*** Sensitive patient-identifying information.

***Anonymization:*** Data cannot be linked to a specific person.

***De-identification:*** Removal of identifying patient information.

***Methods:***
- **Safe Harbor:** Remove 18 specified identifiers.
- **Statistical Method:** Verify very low re-identification risk.
- **Hiding in Plain Sight:** Replace identifiers with realistic substitutes.

## Medical Images

***Definition:*** 2D or 3D arrays of intensity values representing body structures or functions.

***Types:***
- **Structural:** Shows anatomy.
- **Functional:** Shows activity or changes over time.

***Modalities:*** X-ray/CT, ultrasound, MRI, photographs.

***Uses:*** Diagnosis, disease staging, treatment monitoring, and surgical guidance.

- **DICOM:** Standard for medical image storage and transmission.
- **PACS:** System for storing and retrieving medical images.

## Healthcare Signals

***Definition:*** Physiological measurements recorded at regular time intervals (time-series data).

***Examples:*** ECG, heart rate, wearable sensor measurements.

***Challenges:*** Sensor accuracy, false alerts, missed conditions, proprietary algorithms, and privacy.

# Module 6: Electronic Phenotyping

## Electronic Phenotyping

***Phenotype:*** A patient characteristic or condition, such as having diabetes.

***Feature:*** Directly measured information, such as blood glucose.

***Electronic Phenotyping:*** Computationally determining whether a patient has a condition using features from electronic medical records.

***Uses:***
- Identify patient cohorts.
- Recruit patients for clinical trials.
- Find similar patients.
- Measure healthcare quality.

***Challenges:*** Incorrect diagnosis codes, delayed recording, and differences between hospitals.

## Two Approaches

### 1. Rule-Based Phenotyping

***Definition:*** Uses expert-defined inclusion and exclusion rules.

- **Inclusion:** Features that must be present.
- **Exclusion:** Features that must not be present.

***Example:*** Identify diabetes using diagnosis codes + medications + abnormal lab results.

***Advantage:*** Interpretable, based on clinical knowledge.

***Disadvantage:*** Requires substantial expert effort.

### 2. Probabilistic Phenotyping

***Definition:*** Uses machine learning to estimate the probability that a patient has a condition.

- Uses labeled patient records for training.
- Predicts disease presence in unseen records.

***Advantage:*** Scalable and can identify complex patterns.

***Disadvantage:*** Requires training data and may be less interpretable.

## Imperfect Labeling

***Definition:*** Training models with approximate or imperfect labels instead of fully verified diagnoses.

***Anchor:*** A reliable indicator suggesting a patient has a phenotype.

- Keywords and diagnosis codes can create initial labels.
- Knowledge graphs help expand relevant keywords.
- Classifiers learn from additional patient features.

***PheKB:*** Repository of electronic phenotype definitions.

***Aphrodite:*** Software for probabilistic phenotyping using imperfect labeling.

# Module 7: Clinical Data Ethics

## Research vs Clinical Practice

***Research:*** Develops new knowledge, AI models, and algorithms.

***Clinical Practice:*** Applies established tools to diagnose, prevent, or treat disease in individual patients.

## Belmont Report – Three Ethical Principles

### 1. Respect for Persons

- Respect patient autonomy and decision-making.
- Protect people with diminished autonomy.
- Obtain **informed consent** when required.

***Informed Consent:*** Patient understands the research, has capacity to decide, and participates voluntarily without coercion.

### 2. Beneficence

- **Maximize benefits.**
- **Minimize harms.**
- Research risks should be reasonable relative to expected benefits.

### 3. Justice

- Fair distribution of research risks and benefits.
- Avoid unfair exclusion and discrimination.

***IRB (Institutional Review Board):*** Committee that reviews research involving human participants.

---

## Ethical Issues in AI Data

***Research Repositories:*** Data collected specifically for research, usually with consent.

***Secondary Data Use:*** Using existing EHR, insurance, or other data for research.

***Consumer Data:*** Wearables, mobile health apps, and genetic testing.

### Consent Approaches

***Broad Consent:*** Permission for future research uses without separately approving each study.

***Waiver of Consent:*** Research may proceed without normal consent when regulatory conditions are satisfied, including minimal risk and impracticability of obtaining consent.

***De-identified Data:*** Can sometimes be used without consent under applicable rules, but re-identification remains an ethical concern.

**Trade-off:** Requiring consent protects autonomy but may reduce dataset size and representation.

## Returning Research Results

***Therapeutic Misconception:*** Confusing research participation with receiving medical treatment.

***Approaches:***
- **Return None:** Maintain separation between research and clinical care.
- **Return All:** Respect participants' access to their information.
- **Intermediate:** Return clinically valid, significant, actionable findings.

**Main Challenge:** Balance patient autonomy against the risk of harm from uncertain results.

## Learning Health System (LHS)

***Definition:*** Healthcare system that continuously learns from patient data and applies findings to improve care.

- **Quality Improvement:** Improve existing patient care.
- **Comparative Effectiveness:** Determine which usual treatment works best.
- **Precision Health:** Identify the best treatment for specific patient groups.

***AI Role:*** Learn from healthcare data and translate new knowledge into better clinical decisions.

***Ethical Goals:*** Respect patients, improve care, reduce inequalities, protect privacy, and remain transparent.
