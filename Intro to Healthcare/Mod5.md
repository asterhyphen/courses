# MODULE 5 — HEALTH CARE PRODUCTS, PRESCRIPTION DRUGS & QUALITY MEASUREMENT

## 1. Health Care Product Regulation

### Definition

Most products and equipment used in medical care are regulated. In the U.S., the FDA is the main regulatory agency.

### Types of Health Care Products

- Medical devices
- Imaging equipment
- Biologic products
- Drugs

### Drugs

Substances intended for use in:

- Diagnosis
- Treatment
- Mitigation
- Cure
- Prevention of disease

### Main Types of Drugs

#### 1. Prescription Drugs

- Require a prescription to obtain.

#### 2. Over-the-Counter (OTC) Drugs

- Do not require a prescription to obtain.


# 2. Prescription Drug Regulation

### FDA Role

- The FDA oversees prescription drugs in the U.S.
- Its specific mandate is to ensure prescription drugs are:
  - Safe
  - Efficacious (they work)
- FDA approval is required before a prescription drug can be sold in the U.S.

## FDA Drug Approval Process

### Step 1: Investigational New Drug Application (IND)

- Drug companies begin the normal approval process by filing an **IND**.
- The FDA reviews the application.
- If approved, human testing can begin.

### Step 2: Phase I Trials

- Small, early-stage trials.
- Test basic things about the drug, including:
  - Safety in humans
  - Dosage

### Step 3: Phase II Trials

- Usually conducted on dozens to a few hundred people with the disease or condition.
- Test:
  - Effectiveness
  - Side effects

### Step 4: Phase III Trials

- Involves thousands of people.
- Gathers more data about:
  - Effectiveness
  - Outcomes
  - Longer-term effects

### Step 5: New Drug Application (NDA)

- If the trials are successful, the company files an **NDA**.
- The FDA reviews the NDA.
- If approved:
  - The drug can be marketed.
  - Doctors can prescribe it.
  - Patients can buy it.

### Step 6: Phase IV / Post-Market Monitoring

- FDA may continue monitoring drugs after they reach the market.
- Used to assess drug performance.
- FDA can regulate:
  - Labeling
  - Manufacturing processes
  - Other aspects of making and selling drugs.

### Important Facts

- Drug approval takes a long time.
- A general estimate given in the module is **9 years**.
- Development can cost many millions or even billions of dollars.

### Easy Flow

**IND → Phase I → Phase II → Phase III → NDA → FDA Approval → Market → Phase IV**


# 3. Patents, Branded Drugs & Generic Drugs

### Patent

- A patent gives an inventor the right to be the only one selling an invention for a period of time.
- This is called **market exclusivity**.

### Branded Drug

- Drug sold by the manufacturer that first brings the drug to market with patent protection.
- Sold as a brand-name product.
- Tends to be more expensive than generics.

### Generic Drug

- Sold by additional manufacturers after the primary manufacturer's patent expires.
- Increased competition often leads to lower prices.

### Basic Sequence

**Patent Begins → FDA Approval → Branded Drug on Market → Patent Expires → Generic Drugs Enter → More Competition → Prices Often Decrease**


# 4. Prescription Drugs & Pharmacies

### Prescription Process

**Physician → Patient → Pharmacy**

- Patients generally need a prescription from a physician or authorized provider.
- The prescription is taken to a pharmacy to be filled.
- Pharmacies may be:
  - Wholesale
  - Retail
  - Online
  - Brick-and-mortar

### Pharmacists

- Have advanced training.
- Are experts in:
  - Different drugs
  - Drug uses
  - Handling drugs


# 5. Formularies

### Definition

A **formulary** is the list of drugs that an insurance company will allow a patient to use and get reimbursed for.

### Important Points

- Formularies can vary between insurers.
- Formularies can be tiered.

### Tiered Formulary

- Drugs are placed into different "tiers".
- Different tiers have different levels of patient cost sharing.
- Insurers can create incentives for patients to use drugs in preferred tiers.

### Other Insurance Controls

#### Pre-authorization

- Patient needs to seek pre-approval from the plan before getting certain medicines.

#### Step Therapy

- The plan may require the patient to try a more preferred drug first.
- A less preferred drug may only be covered after the preferred option has been tried.


# 6. Pharmacy Benefit Managers (PBMs)

### Definition

A **Pharmacy Benefit Manager (PBM)** is a third party that intermediaries commonly contract with to manage prescription drug coverage.

### Three Main Functions of PBMs

1. **Manage prescription drug benefit design**
   - Formularies
   - Networks

2. **Manage payments to pharmacies**

3. **Negotiate**
   - Prices with pharmacies
   - Rebates with manufacturers

### Rebates

- Rebates are important when understanding prescription drug prices.
- The retail prices paid by PBMs to pharmacies may or may not represent the actual amount an insurer paid after rebates.


# 7. Pharmacy Switch

### Definition

A **pharmacy switch** is a third-party vendor used by pharmacists to transmit claims from the pharmacy to the PBMs.

### Functions

- Helps determine who pays what.
- Determines patient cost sharing, such as copays.
- Identifies which PBM handles the patient's coverage.
- Gets cost-sharing and other coverage information electronically from the PBM.
- Sends the information back to the pharmacy.


# 8. Data & Innovation in Prescription Drugs

### Drug Discovery & FDA Approval

Opportunities include:

- Finding new drug targets.
- Identifying new areas for research.
- Working with data and studies involved in approval processes.

### Insurance & Payment

Innovation can help:

- Manage drug use.
- Make better use of drugs when there are different choices.
- Identify patients who are the best candidates for particular drugs.

### Data Sources

- Insurers
- Pharmacies
- PBMs
- Pharmacy switches

These organizations may provide different perspectives on what is happening in prescription drug use and payment.


# 9. Quality Measurement & Improvement

## Six Domains of Quality — STEEEP

1. **Safe**
2. **Timely**
3. **Effective**
4. **Efficient**
5. **Equitable**
6. **Patient-centered**

### STEEEP

- **S** = Safe
- **T** = Timely
- **E** = Effective
- **E** = Efficient
- **E** = Equitable
- **P** = Patient-centered


# 10. Overuse, Underuse & Misuse

### Overuse

- The healthcare system uses too much healthcare.

### Underuse

- The healthcare system does not use enough healthcare.

### Misuse

- Healthcare tools are used in the wrong way or at the wrong time.

### Framework

**Overuse ↔ Underuse ↔ Misuse**


# 11. Donabedian Model — Structure, Process & Outcome

The **Structure-Process-Outcome** framework is also called the **Donabedian model**.

### 1. Structure

Looks at the conditions and resources available to provide care.

Examples:

- Facilities
- Staff
- Equipment
- Infrastructure
- Provider qualifications

### 2. Process

Looks at how care is delivered.

Examples:

- Whether appropriate guidelines are followed.
- Whether recommended treatments are provided.

### 3. Outcome

Looks at the results of healthcare.

Examples:

- Patient health
- Mortality
- Readmissions
- Complications

### Easy Memory

**Structure = What you have**

**Process = What you do**

**Outcome = What happens**


# 12. Levels of Quality Measurement

Quality can be measured for:

1. **Providers or groups of providers**
2. **Health plans or intermediaries**
3. **Individuals or groups of patients**


# 13. Quality Measurement — Structure

### Main Question

**Does the healthcare system have a structure conducive to delivering quality care?**

### Structural Questions

- Are there enough providers?
- Are providers in the right locations?
- Do providers have appropriate training and skills?
- Is equipment available?
- Is infrastructure organized and financed so that it can work effectively?

### Structural Measures for Providers

1. Is a good **EMR/EHR** system in place?
2. Are emergency protocols documented?
3. Are protocols to prevent the spread of infections in place?
4. Are physicians and other providers appropriately credentialed?
5. Are facilities appropriately:
   - Accredited
   - Licensed
   - Certified

### Structural Measures for Health Plans or Populations

- Are there enough physicians and other professionals?
- Are there enough hospital beds?
- Where are the beds located relative to the population?

### Limitation

- Structural measures are often easier to construct.
- However, they may be farther from actual health outcomes.


# 14. Quality Measurement — Process

### Definition

**Process measures** examine whether appropriate healthcare processes are being followed.

### Main Idea

If there is an outcome that needs to be achieved:

1. Identify processes that should lead to that outcome.
2. Measure whether those processes occur.
3. Track how often they occur.

### Common Approach

Measure whether generally accepted recommendations for clinical practice are followed.

This can involve:

- Defining a population of patients.
- Identifying a treatment or procedure supported by evidence.
- Measuring the share of the population receiving the recommended service.

### Process Measures Can Identify

- Care that **should be provided** but is not.
- Care that **should not be provided** but is.

### Common Data Sources

- EMR databases
- Claims databases
- Registries

### Registry

A database that collects and compiles information about many patients with a given condition or patients receiving a particular treatment.

### Important

**Process measures are probably the most common form of quality measure.**


# 15. Quality Measurement — Outcomes

### Definition

Outcome measures examine the **results of healthcare**.

### Main Questions

- How is the patient's health status affected?
- How is quality of life affected?
- How is life expectancy affected?
- Did behavior change?
- Did knowledge improve?

### Examples of Outcome Measures

1. **Mortality Rate**
2. **Readmission Rate**
3. **Complication Rate**
4. **Potentially Preventable Admissions**
   - Also called ambulatory care-sensitive admissions.
5. **Patient-Reported Outcome Measures**
   - **PROMs**


# 16. Risk Adjustment

### Why Is It Needed?

Different providers or health plans may care for different types of patients.

Differences in outcomes may result from:

- Differences in actual quality.
- Differences in patient characteristics.
- Factors beyond the control of providers or plans.

### Definition

**Risk adjustment** = Approaches designed to correct for differing characteristics of patients.

### Key Idea

**Different Patient Characteristics → Risk Adjustment → More Meaningful Outcome Measurement**


# 17. Quality Improvement

Quality improvement requires selecting the appropriate set of measures.

### Three Main Types

- Structure measures
- Process measures
- Outcome measures

These measures can be useful individually or in combination.


# 18. HEDIS

### HEDIS = Healthcare Effectiveness Data and Information Set

- Developed by the **National Center for Quality Assurance**.
- Used for measuring quality of health plans.
- Includes:
  - Structure measures
  - Process measures
  - Outcome measures
- Can be used to compare health plans.


# 19. Who Measures Healthcare Quality?

### Government

- Government organizations may measure quality.
- Example: Medicare has developed tools for measuring the quality of healthcare providers.

### Healthcare Providers

- Hospitals and physician practices may measure their own quality.
- Providers may assess their own performance.

### Quality Organizations

- Organizations working to improve quality can develop quality measurement systems.


# 20. Uses of Quality Data

Quality data can be used for:

1. **Monitoring and improving provider quality**
2. **Public reporting**
3. **Designing payment incentives**


# 21. AI & Data Opportunities in Healthcare Quality

AI and data can support:

- Measuring healthcare quality.
- Measuring safety and effectiveness.
- Improving equity.
- Improving patient-centered care.
- Risk adjustment.
- Guiding treatment choices.
- Identifying appropriate providers.
- Designing networks for payers.
- Designing incentives linked to quality measures.
